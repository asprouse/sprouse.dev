# Leadership & Working Style

How I actually run things — hiring, feedback, decisions, conflict, communication, team rhythm, and what changes when engineers work alongside coding agents every day.

## Hiring

<!-- id: lead-1 | tags: [hiring, philosophy] -->
## 1. Your hiring philosophy in one sentence?

Hire the person, not the resume. Personality, attitude, motivation, and solid reasoning matter more than individual experiences on the resume. The goal is to identify a talented individual who is eager to grow into the role and is a pleasure to work with.

<!-- id: lead-2 | tags: [hiring, signal] -->
## 2. The single most important thing you look for in a candidate?

A potential hire needs to have curiosity that fuels their ability to learn on the job. We will always be working with unknowns. The candidate needs to be comfortable with not knowing everything, and have a solid framework for researching and developing solutions in an ever-changing landscape.

<!-- id: lead-3 | tags: [hiring, process] -->
## 3. How do you actually run an interview — what's your structure?

It depends on the role and how well I know their body of work. First I want to know their background — how they got to this point and why they're looking for a new gig.

Then we cover the resume and have them talk through their most recent project: why they made certain decisions, their thought process, and challenges they had to overcome. I want to understand how they approach problems — especially problems with incomplete information.

If necessary, we'll cover some technical questions. Then I talk about our company and what we're looking for, and what the opportunity looks like. Finally, I open it up for questions.

<!-- id: lead-4 | tags: [hiring, intuition, redflags] -->
## 4. A red flag that's hard to articulate but you trust?

Over-confidence in solutions. Specifically:

- Using superlatives like "X is the best LLM for Y."
- Inability or unwillingness to discuss tradeoffs in design decisions.
- Terseness.

<!-- id: lead-5 | tags: [hiring, resume, signal] -->
## 5. How do you read a resume in 2026 — what gets your attention?

I don't think this has changed recently. I look for continuity, and how they've learned from their experience and progressed through their career.

## Feedback & Performance

<!-- id: lead-6 | tags: [feedback, hard-conversations] -->
## 6. How do you give hard feedback?

First, I need to prepare for the meeting. I need to have direct, constructive feedback with clear action items before going into the conversation. In the meeting, we start with things that are positive, then transition to the hard feedback, then next steps and how the feedback can be addressed. The worst thing a manager can do is be unclear.

<!-- id: lead-7 | tags: [feedback, receiving] -->
## 7. How do you receive hard feedback?

Hard feedback is never a pleasant experience to receive. I want to make sure I fully understand the issues, and that I have actual steps to fix them before I leave the meeting. I also want to understand an objective measure I can use to track my progress.

<!-- id: lead-8 | tags: [performance, hard-conversations, formative] -->
## 8. The hardest performance conversation you've ever had?

I feel like the rare times my performance has dipped have usually coincided with personal matters interfering with my ability to put out my best work. In these conversations, the other person usually doesn't say much — the harshest feedback comes from my inner dialogue.

<!-- id: lead-9 | tags: [growth, evaluation] -->
## 9. How do you tell whether someone's actually growing vs. just busy?

As an engineer, there are two ways to grow: breadth or depth. Either is valuable depending on the circumstances. I look for engineers to proactively put forth demos, initiate technical design conversations, and not color in the box.

## Decision-Making

<!-- id: lead-10 | tags: [decisions, team] -->
## 10. Walk me through how you make a hard call when the team is split.

As an engineering organization, I always want to lead with evidence. Agentic coding has made doing research spikes and benchmarks easier than ever. Data might obviate the decision if opinions are split.

When all things are equal, I want to understand why each side has their preferences. Using a framework like the "5 whys" helps get to the root of things. Then the hard part is choosing the solution: either the one that's more consistent with the current approach, or the one with the merits of a new direction.

<!-- id: lead-11 | tags: [decisions, delegation] -->
## 11. When do you defer to a teammate vs. override?

I think this is situational, but I try to gauge who is in the best position to make the call. If I have personal experience with the problem at hand and there's a certain smell that sets off red flags, I'll override. That said, that's the minority of the time. Usually a teammate has been marinating in that domain longer than I have, and is in a better position to make the call.

<!-- id: lead-12 | tags: [decisions, growth] -->
## 12. A decision pattern you've gotten better at over time?

Clarity. In tickets, I want to make it clear what decisions have already been made and what needs work. When things are unclear or need research, be explicit that doing the research and coming up with a solution is part of the task.

I find that doing proof-of-concept spikes — the kind that enable us to write the spec for the production version — is a better pattern than trying to one-shot a project from a loose user story.

<!-- id: lead-13 | tags: [decisions, weaknesses] -->
## 13. A decision pattern you still get wrong?

I'd say I'm eager to release features and get them into production. Sometimes you need to cut into it to determine that the turkey needs more time in the oven. Nailing the "minimal" part of MVP is an ever-present challenge — startups don't have the luxury of polishing products to perfection like, say, a company like Apple does. Getting the balance right is hard, and it's a muscle I work to strengthen.

## Conflict & Communication

<!-- id: lead-14 | tags: [conflict, team] -->
## 14. How do you handle conflict between teammates?

When tensions are high, I start with personal respect. I communicate that I first and foremost respect them as a person, and that the only thing we should be critical of is the work itself. If a line has been crossed, I start with an apology.

Once emotions have subsided, I want to listen and learn about their perspective, and then share my own. Most of the time we can fall back to an objective problem-solving framework and move on.

<!-- id: lead-15 | tags: [communication, advocacy] -->
## 15. The communication habit you wish more engineers had?

Engineers are critical by nature and love to trash-talk. Sometimes the criticism is pointed inward. I wish folks made it clear when they need to vent and blow off steam. Too often, venting is misinterpreted by the team as actual questions rather than rhetorical ones. Either make it clear you're venting, or present problems with a path to a solution.

<!-- id: lead-16 | tags: [communication, growth, self-criticism] -->
## 16. The communication habit you've worked hardest to break in yourself?

Reactive communication vs proactive. Early in my career I'd undercommunicate to a fault. I wanted to prove to my bosses and peers that I was a competent participant. I'd wait for the review to ask questions, and by then my assumptions had created extra work.

I continually work to be proactive in communication: ask questions, set expectations, work toward alignment. Earlier is better, and overcommunicating is better than under.

<!-- id: lead-17 | tags: [cofounder, mark, disagreement] -->
## 17. How do you and Mark disagree productively after this many years?

After many years working together, we know how each other communicates best — how to productively give feedback in a way that's constructive and well-received. We set up weekly check-ins every Monday morning to discuss issues, and we set aside additional time if necessary. Prioritizing these discussions has been key to our collaboration.

## Remote, In-Person, and Rhythm

<!-- id: lead-18 | tags: [remote, rhythm] -->
## 18. TakeShape's rhythm — fully remote, in-person, hybrid? Why?

We're fully remote, but that wasn't the original intent. We had been looking at office spaces in late 2019 / early 2020 when the pandemic threw a monkey wrench into that plan. By the time the pandemic had subsided there weren't enough of us in the same location to make a shared office make sense — Mark moved back to Boston for family reasons.

Ideally we'd do a hybrid approach — I'd say 75/25 in-office vs remote. Mark and I both found that being in a shared space during our time at Techstars was the most productive. Having flexibility to travel and work remote is a key contributor to work-life balance.

<!-- id: lead-19 | tags: [team, rituals] -->
## 19. The most underrated team ritual?

As a fully remote company, we rarely get to spend time together in person. Coming together for an offsite — like a conference — has been invaluable for team morale and shared vision. I wish we were able to do more.

<!-- id: lead-20 | tags: [meetings, anti-pattern] -->
## 20. A meeting you've killed and don't miss?

Most of the meetings I've killed have been customer-facing meetings that were unnecessary. Instead, we use a shared Slack channel to reduce the need for calls to discuss things that would be better off as a Slack message or email.

Internally we've kept our recurring meetings to a minimum: daily standup, weekly pointing, monthly retro. Other meetings are held only when necessary, and require preparation. The prep requirement: the host must document the meeting agenda and what they want to present beforehand, so attendees can pre-read and the time together is spent mostly in discussion. This requirement alone eliminates many unnecessary meetings — because during preparation, you might answer your own questions.

## Leading Through AI

<!-- id: lead-21 | tags: [ai, leadership, daily] -->
## 21. How do you lead a team where engineers are working with coding agents daily?

In 2026 this is certainly a new challenge, but I feel like the basics still apply — except now each team member has agents to delegate to. I find that the approval process for PRs is the most impacted, since that's the thing that ultimately gets flattened.

We currently do a two-step review process. When the PR is opened, we have an agent do the preliminary review — Claude or Copilot. After that round of feedback has been addressed, we tag in a human reviewer. Using agents to help review the additional number of PRs the team generates is critical — it helps streamline the human bottleneck. Teams who optimize their human-in-the-loop workflows will be the most productive.

<!-- id: lead-22 | tags: [ai, leadership, failure-modes] -->
## 22. The new failure mode that came with AI-augmented engineering?

The new failure mode is "AI slop" — the LLM generates so much that it's impractical to review or QA. PR comments the length of novels. Code comments that explain the obvious. Implementations that miss the mark.

It's a new problem: determining the best way to control the agent's output, and how to encode unwritten rules into AGENTS.md or skills. We also need to be deliberate in our product process to make sure agents are working on high-priority tasks. It's a similar problem to having a larger team: how to effectively parallelize and prioritize your high-value tasks.

<!-- id: lead-23 | tags: [leadership, ai, growth] -->
## 23. A leadership skill that's *more* important now than three years ago?

The ability to delegate. Maybe in the past a leader could afford to be a 10xer and carve the path for the team — leading by example and showing how they want things to be done. We now need to learn how to delegate the process of figuring out *what* to do. Agents should be involved in your preparation as well as execution, and those who can delegate the most effectively will come out on top.

<!-- id: lead-24 | tags: [leadership, ai, deprecation] -->
## 24. A leadership skill that matters less now?

I'd say that your contributions as an IC are less important.
