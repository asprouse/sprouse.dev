# Origin & Arc

How I got here — childhood through TakeShape, with the *why* behind each move. Answers can be as short as a sentence or as long as a story; whatever feels honest.

<!-- id: origin-1 | tags: [childhood, formative] -->
## 1. Where did you grow up, and what was the first thing you ever built — code or otherwise?

I grew up in Stonington, Connecticut. The first thing I ever built was a HyperCard game where you were a duck with a plunger gun. You'd shoot a dog, and when the plunger hit, the dog would flip upside down. I used HyperTalk to animate the buttons — each one had multiple icons that switched when you pressed the arrow keys.

<!-- id: origin-2 | tags: [childhood, formative] -->
## 2. What got you into computers in the first place? A specific moment, or a slow drift?

As a kid I always wanted to create video games. I got a Game Boy when I was five — brand new at the time — and fell in love with Mario and Metroid. Naturally, my first creation was a HyperCard game.

<!-- id: origin-3 | tags: [education, decision] -->
## 3. How did you choose Northeastern, and how did you settle on CS once you were there?

I always knew I wanted to go to school for computer science. I got into other schools that were probably higher ranked — Rensselaer, for one — but they were in the middle of nowhere upstate. I'd always liked the idea of living in a city, so I picked Boston and Northeastern.

<!-- id: origin-4 | tags: [education, formative] -->
## 4. Co-op culture is core to Northeastern. How much did your co-ops shape what kind of engineer you became?

Co-ops were invaluable — they taught me what being an engineer was actually like. School taught me theory; co-op taught me software engineering. Across my three co-ops I worked my way up:

1. **Bose**, as a QA person.
2. **MIT Lincoln Laboratories**, building websites for the benchmarking team.
3. **Virage**, where I became a full-stack Java developer.

<!-- id: origin-5 | tags: [pfizer, first-job, formative] -->
## 5. The Pfizer summer in 2004 was your first paid coding job. What's the moment from that summer you still remember?

This was my first taste of a desk job. I worked with the DBA team and wrote a web app to track DBAs on call. It was built on a custom in-house Java framework called Web Wingnut. The frontend was JavaScript with lots of frames to make the UI feel dynamic — this was before AJAX was a thing.

I was just a kid, and all the guys I worked with were Indian. They were surprised I didn't have a wife. Every lunchtime they'd have these elaborate meals prepared for them, and they'd make fun of me for having nobody to cook for me.

This was the first place I realized people are realistic rather than idealistic. They'd take shortcuts because of the time they had; they weren't always perfectionists, and they knowingly made those trade-offs.

<!-- id: origin-6 | tags: [bose, formative, habits] -->
## 6. The Bose tester gig in 2005 — you've said it taught you spec writing and issue reporting. What's a habit from that role you still use 20 years later?

Being QA at Bose instilled in me how important testing is in a product development cycle. They were building hardware — no second chances, no automatic patches and updates. They had to get it right the first time.

The habit I picked up: always thinking about how to poke holes in a spec, identifying the edge cases as I'm writing, and making sure those edge cases are covered in tests.

<!-- id: origin-7 | tags: [mit-lincoln, education] -->
## 7. MIT Lincoln Lab in 2006 was a short stint. What got you in, and why didn't it stick?

MIT Lincoln Laboratory was a four-month co-op, so it naturally ended after four months. It was a cool experience — a DOD-funded research laboratory. To access the same rooms my boss used while doing the benchmarking, I had to obtain my secret clearance.

<!-- id: origin-8 | tags: [autonomy, decision] -->
## 8. Autonomy was your first full engineering job out of college. How did that hire happen, and what excited you about it at the time?

My final co-op was at Virage/Autonomy. I guess I impressed them during my four-month stint — they wanted me to stay on through my final semester and had a job offer lined up for as soon as I graduated.

The most interesting story is how I got hired there in the first place. Initially I didn't get a callback after sending in my resume — I wasn't the best at representing myself on paper. I went to my co-op advisor and said, "I really only want to take a serious software engineering role for my final co-op. I don't want to settle for QA or work for a company that isn't really building serious software." My advisor called up David at Virage and said, "Hey, would you mind giving Andrew a second look?"

I got an interview — a four-on-one with a series of whiteboard challenges, the most thorough interview I'd ever done. Leaving it, they really got to see who I was: that I had a sense of humor, that I knew the things on my resume and more. I'd probably underrepresented myself. On the walk home, I got a call offering me the job.

<!-- id: origin-9 | tags: [autonomy, learning] -->
## 9. Three-plus years at Autonomy spanning video search, OSINT, internationalization, and admin'ing a server rack. What did you walk out of there knowing how to do that you didn't know going in?

This was the first time I worked as a Java developer on a team with multiple people. I learned how to contribute to a shared repo, how to branch and merge, and how to collaborate with other engineers via software. This was before GitHub — we used SVN with Trac on top of it.

This job also taught me a lot about responsibility. Being the admin of your own app really instills a sense of ownership. My boss, David, would go into the server room in the morning and unplug an ethernet cable just to keep you on your toes. You'd have to go in, check that your server was running, and diagnose all the way down to the hardware — down to the ethernet switch.

The same applied to the code we wrote. The features I added were being used by real users — real government users — and it mattered that they worked. If something went wrong I was the go-to person to consult and fix. It really helped me mature as an engineer and as a software owner.

<!-- id: origin-10 | tags: [boston, nyc, geography] -->
## 10. What was Boston like for you in your early career, and what eventually pulled you to New York?

Boston was great. That's where I went to school at Northeastern, and it was a nice place to live — lots of young people, lots of people my age. After graduation a lot of my friends stuck around and got jobs there.

About three years out, folks were moving on. Boston is a college town, and even in your 20s you start to feel the separation from being a student — you're in a kind of no-man's-land between professional and student. My social life stagnated. The move to New York was really a way to jump-start it again — a forcing function to meet new people and grow my career.

<!-- id: origin-11 | tags: [newsweek, transition, decision] -->
## 11. The jump from Autonomy to Newsweek/Daily Beast in 2010 was a big context switch — defense-adjacent enterprise to a newsroom. What drove the move?

My friend and future co-founder, Mark, was working at Newsweek at the time. We'd gone to school together at Northeastern, and he had moved to New York a year before. When I let him know I was looking for a gig and wanted to move to New York, he said Newsweek was looking for Java engineers.

It was a new domain, but the architecture was very similar to what we used for tracking open-source intelligence. They were using a database (JCR) plus a search index (Solr) to make article lookups performant. At Autonomy we'd had a pipeline built on MySQL and Autonomy's proprietary IDOL.

<!-- id: origin-12 | tags: [newsweek, leadership, formative] -->
## 12. You progressed engineer → senior → lead at Newsweek over three years. Was there a specific moment you knew you were ready to lead?

When I joined Newsweek, I was one of the most experienced Java developers on the team. My time at Autonomy — a software company — gave me the confidence to lead, especially with people who weren't necessarily coming from as rigorous a background.

In those days the New York tech scene was much less mature than it is today. Being from Boston, a much more tech-minded city, gave me an edge. I had confidence in my technical ability, so leading came naturally — folks would come to me with questions, and I fell into the role.

<!-- id: origin-13 | tags: [newsweek, lessons, culture] -->
## 13. What did working in a newsroom teach you that working at a software company didn't?

Working in a newsroom taught me a lot about product development. The lesson: you can build the most technically amazing software in the world, but if your users don't know how to use it — or it doesn't match their workflow — you're wasting your time. It taught me how to collaborate with non-technical users, do requirements gathering, and iterate toward something they actually needed and enjoyed using.

One example: I worked on a photo gallery component for the photo editors. They were spending huge amounts of time uploading photos at exact dimensions — doing the resizing manually in Photoshop. We were able to automatically generate the different sizes and variations and save them a ton of time. They didn't even know that was possible.

The existing product team did a lot of top-down dictating of what the publication's producers would use. But there were many cases where the engineering team collaborated directly with the end user to make sure we got it right. That kind of direct feedback is something I value — and something we've always tried to do at TakeShape.

<!-- id: origin-14 | tags: [pixel-forensics, transition] -->
## 14. The Pixel Forensics contracting stretch in 2013 was short — what was the story there?

Pixel Forensics was during a stretch when I was trying to start my own software development shop, and they were one of my clients. They had a contract with the NBA — an application that would analyze broadcast footage and track the position of players and the ball, giving NBA teams data on their play to make improvements.

I worked on the frontend: a frame-by-frame annotation UI where the automated system would suggest where it thought the players and ball were at a given moment, and the user could go in and make corrections. This, along with my work at Autonomy, gave me exposure to machine learning and the idea that robust, manually curated datasets are how you produce highly accurate models.

<!-- id: origin-15 | tags: [ronik, agency, decision] -->
## 15. You joined Ronik Design in 2013. What attracted you to an agency after three years in media?

Mark and two designers had left Newsweek to form Ronik Design. While I was doing contract work for Pixel Forensics, they hired me to do contract work for them on various projects. My role as Director of Technology at Ronik was just formalizing that contract work into a real position at the agency.

<!-- id: origin-16 | tags: [fair-tread, founding, matt-mankins] -->
## 16. Fair Tread (2015) was your first co-founder gig with Matt Mankins. How did you two meet, and how did the conversation become a company?

After Newsweek, Mark moved on to Fast Company magazine, where Matt Mankins was the CTO. I met Matt through Mark at Ronik — he'd come in from time to time to share what he was working on.

I worked at Ronik for a couple of years and then left to pursue a more dedicated software engineering gig. While I was looking for contract work, Matt invited me to Berlin for two weeks to help him take an idea and push it out into the world — that idea was Fair Tread. My payment was a plane ticket and an Airbnb.

At the end of the trip, Matt mentioned he was living in Amsterdam but moving back to New York to keep working on this and other startup ideas. When he moved back, we ended up working together. He was a mentor at the 2015 Techstars class in New York, and we took a table in the corner of the office — that's where we built Fair Tread.

<!-- id: origin-17 | tags: [fair-tread, x402, hindsight] -->
## 17. HTTP 402 micropayments was nearly a decade early. What's it been like watching x402 emerge years later?

I'm glad that in 2026 this idea is gaining momentum. The x402 stuff is pretty encouraging, but it's yet to break through and have mainstream adoption. My parents, for example, don't pay for things with x402 — but it's the right direction.

In 2015 we were pre-mainstream blockchain, and micropayments have only gotten more technically attainable since. This is a problem that relies on a huge platform and network effects. It wasn't realistic to expect that our work on 402 would lead to mass adoption — this is the kind of thing that takes many companies collaborating, lots of iteration over the years, before an idea permeates the zeitgeist. I'm pretty proud that our ideas are at least being validated.

<!-- id: origin-18 | tags: [fair-tread, failure, lessons] -->
## 18. What were the actual reasons Fair Tread wound down, in your own words?

It's pretty simple: we ran out of money. Fair Tread was fully bootstrapped by Matt and me. We had offers — namely from the leading ad-block plug-in company — to take a seed round, but we worried it was a strategic investment on their part. One of the main features of the Fair Tread 402 paywall was using ad-block-detecting JavaScript to enforce a paywall on the server side, and we were worried they were just investing to keep tabs on us and potentially shut us down.

Adoption of our server-side middleware paywall was slow because of the infrastructure change it required from potential customers. They also wanted to see a pre-existing network effect across other sites taking our 402 payment, and we couldn't get an initial cohort to all sign on together. I think we set our sights too high — Matt was very well connected in the New York media scene and we wanted a flagship media property to sign on.

We ultimately tried to garner attention with atri.me to show traction for the micropayments idea to potential customers and investors. After we launched atri.me and ran the experiment for a while, there were disagreements about the direction the company should take. We chose to go our separate ways amicably.

<!-- id: origin-19 | tags: [ronik, decision] -->
## 19. Going back to Ronik in 2016 is an unusual move. Why return, and what was different the second time?

In the summer of 2016 I approached Mark and Ronik and pitched them the idea of an API-first content management system. It was a pet idea Mark and I had during our previous stint working together at Ronik. We'd been putting clients on CMSs like WordPress, and instead of using the WordPress frontend, we'd build our own using static HTML, JavaScript, and the WordPress APIs.

We saw the potential in static site generation — what it meant for performance and user experience — and wanted a platform where the data model was flexible. We could have an API that we could use to build a static site in a clean and user-friendly way.

The pitch was: we'd build this product (which didn't have a name yet) in-house, use it on client projects to test and incubate it, and then potentially spin it out and get investors. I had learned enough about serverless technology and how startups worked at Fair Tread that I had confidence in the plan.

<!-- id: origin-20 | tags: [takeshape, founding] -->
## 20. The Ronik-to-TakeShape spinout in 2018 — when did you and your co-founder know there was a real company in there, not just a useful internal tool?

It's a bit of a chicken-and-egg question. We created the product out of our own experience and started developing it with the mission of building a real product and company. From day one we thought of it as a SaaS platform, not just a piece of software we wrote into a customer's repo.

What changed in 2018 is that we had a number of successful projects running on TakeShape. We saw initial product-market fit, and what we needed was funding to grow the team and build faster.

<!-- id: origin-21 | tags: [techstars, founding] -->
## 21. Techstars 2019. What's the unvarnished version of that experience?

In 2019 we got admitted to Techstars in Philadelphia. We'd had difficulty in 2018 raising money on our own and realized we needed support and education around the business side of startups.

Part of the reasoning for going through Techstars was that while our product had product-market fit with our initial customers, investors were telling us the headless CMS space was already saturated and we weren't differentiated enough. In 2018 our differentiator was GraphQL-based static site generation, but most of the community wanted external static site generators like Gatsby. After attending the Jamstack conference and listening to customers, we realized there was a new problem in the headless space: now that every feature was available as an API-first SaaS product, how does one compose them all together to actually build a business on top? In 2019 we entered Techstars with the prototype of a TakeShape API mesh product to help users compose their APIs.

The Techstars experience was invaluable. It was what enabled us to raise money. We learned how to pitch, how to market ourselves, how to email prospective investors and research them — it was our miniature MBA. We met a lot of fellow founders, and the camaraderie was really what carried us through the challenging three months.

I got married in May of 2019, and a month later Mark and I moved from Brooklyn to Philadelphia for Techstars. I told myself that if I was going to be away from my new wife, I had to make it worth it. We lived college-style in a small row house in south Philadelphia, woke up at 6 a.m., and rode our bikes into the office every morning. We were usually the first people there, getting in at 7 a.m. and often staying till 10 p.m. It felt good to work really, really hard at something, and it was amazing to see other founders doing the same side by side.

A big takeaway from Techstars was the idea of "big rocks." The metaphor: if you fill your glass with sand and small rocks first, there's no room left for the big, important things. So when you're filling your time, you put the things you'd be crazy not to do in first — the little particulars fill the cracks around them, and you're always working on what's most important.

Following Techstars, we were able to raise money in 2020 — right before the global pandemic.

<!-- id: origin-22 | tags: [career, philosophy, throughline] -->
## 22. Across all these jobs, is there a through-line — a problem you keep being drawn back to?

Across all these jobs, there's one through-line: building products. Building products that people like to use, that make their jobs easier — that's the most rewarding part of being an engineer.

These jobs helped me figure out what that process should look like. Early on it was: come up with a hypothesis of what the user would like, build it, then test it. By Techstars I'd learned about lean startup methodology — be smarter with your time, figure out what folks actually want first, then build it. It's been a journey.

<!-- id: origin-23 | tags: [career, hindsight, formative] -->
## 23. Which job felt like a mistake at the time but turned out to be formative?

Working at Fair Tread felt like a mistake. It felt like I had wasted a lot of time and money trying to bootstrap a business. In the end I learned a lot from it.

I learned that if you have an idea, you can just make a business out of it — and that really motivated me to be a future founder. Matt was also very enthusiastic about serverless technology and the potential it had as a foundation to build on. He got me kicking and screaming into using Lambda in 2015, when there was no tooling around it and the developer experience was horrible. Now I realize how forward-thinking it was, and I'm glad I learned how to use such a powerful technology so early on.

<!-- id: origin-24 | tags: [takeshape, growth, leadership] -->
## 24. You've been at TakeShape seven-plus years. How is the version of you running it today different from the version that started it?

It's interesting because the first version of TakeShape was incubated at Ronik, where we did professional services for customers and built websites on top of the prototype version of TakeShape. We had that formative experience and then moved on to a bottoms-up approach: attract developers to use the product, and their companies would end up paying for it. We were able to do the developer-first approach because we'd already figured out product-market fit working for our customers — we were simply able to duplicate that success by inviting more developers into our circle.

Now that we're building an agent framework and runtime, we've returned to professional services as a way to validate our own ideas on real customer projects. In 2026 we're also working toward developer adoption by creating an MCP and plugins to let users "vibe-code" with TakeShape.

Putting the company aside: in the past 10 years a lot of things have changed. I've gotten married, I've had a child — I've changed a lot personally. The 2016 version of Andrew was probably more idealistic, more engineering-focused than the current version. In 2026 I know the value of marketing and sales, and I understand that if you build it, they won't come. You need to align yourself with people who complement the strengths you have and the ones you don't. All that said: my core values haven't changed, and the genuine desire to make cool things that people love persists.

<!-- id: origin-25 | tags: [career, philosophy] -->
## 25. Twenty years in — what still surprises you about software?

What surprises me about software are the cycles — how much they resemble the planet's weather cycles. We see the same trends oscillate back and forth. Strongly typed languages like C and Java, then duck-typed languages like Ruby and JavaScript, then back to TypeScript. Or in our own experience: monolithic software splitting out into microservices, then microservices coming back together behind an API gateway to create a new monolith.

These cycles will repeat over and over again. I initially thought software progress would be linear, but I realize each generation has to learn similar lessons over and over. It's very rare for people to have a bird's-eye view that lets them skip the latest fad. I hope my 20 years have given me a bit of that perspective — and that it makes me a valuable contributor as more and more of coding gets taken over by AI agents.
